A CMAP is a sum of motor units

When you stimulate the median nerve at the wrist and record over the thumb, you get one smooth waveform: the compound muscle action potential, or CMAP. It is the sum of about 200 motor unit potentials, one for each motor axon, all arriving within a few milliseconds.

On the screen, each coloured band is one motor unit. The colour shows how fast that axon conducts, from amber (slow) to blue (fast). The dark line is the total.

Recruiting motor units

Start by . You get a single, small motor unit potential. Then and watch more units join in.

The fast (blue) axons join first, because large axons have the lowest threshold to electrical stimulation. This is the opposite of a voluntary contraction, where small motor units fire first.

Supramaximal stimulation

Once every axon is firing, the amplitude stops growing. Labs then turn the current up about 20% more to be sure. If the stimulus is too weak (), the CMAP is small and can be mistaken for axon loss.

What the numbers mean

Amplitude reflects how many motor units respond and how large they are.

Latency is measured to the first deflection. That point is set by the few fastest axons, not the average. In the dot plot, the dashed line lines up with the first blue dots.

For residents

Why do you need two sites to measure velocity?

The distal motor latency includes more than conduction along the nerve. It also includes slower conduction in the fine terminal branches, about 1 ms for transmission across the neuromuscular junction, and time for the muscle fibres to activate. Together these make up the residual latency.

If you stimulate at two sites and subtract the two latencies, everything the two paths share cancels out. What remains is the travel time along the nerve between the sites. Velocity is the distance divided by that time.

Sensory studies have no synapse, so one site is enough.

Why do demyelinated nerves need more current?

Damaged myelin raises the threshold for stimulation. In CIDP or CMT1A you may need 50–100 mA or a longer pulse. If a deep proximal site is under-stimulated, the response is falsely small and can look like conduction block.

In this simulator, each axon's threshold rises with how demyelinated it is at the stimulation site.

Amplitude or area?

Area measures the whole negative phase, so it is less affected by changes in shape. Many conduction block criteria use area for this reason. Amplitude is easier to measure and is reported more often.

Distance spreads the units out

Each axon conducts at a slightly different speed. Over the short distance from wrist to thumb, fast and slow axons arrive at nearly the same time. From the axilla, the slow axons arrive several milliseconds after the fast ones.

The screen shows one nerve stimulated at three sites. Compare the colours: the blue units stay early, and the amber units fall further behind at each site.

Phase cancellation

Each motor unit potential has an upward (negative) phase followed by a downward (positive) phase. When units arrive at different times, the downward phase of an early unit overlaps the upward phase of a later one, and they partly cancel. The CMAP becomes smaller and longer, even though no axon has failed. This is called phase cancellation.

The two-unit panel shows this with just two units. : the sum is only a little bigger than one unit. At , the second unit's peak lines up with the first unit's trough.

SNAPs are affected more

. A single sensory fibre potential lasts about 1 ms, compared with about 8 ms for a motor unit. The same timing differences therefore cancel much more of it. In a normal nerve, the SNAP can drop by half between wrist and elbow, while the CMAP drops by only 10–15%. The distance plot shows both.

Demyelination increases the spread

Acquired demyelination slows some fibres much more than others. and the proximal CMAP gets lower and longer. This is abnormal temporal dispersion. It can lower the amplitude enough to look like conduction block.

For residents

How do you tell block from dispersion?

Look at the duration. In conduction block, some units are missing, so the amplitude falls but the duration stays about the same. In dispersion, all units are present but spread out, so the amplitude falls and the duration increases.

That is why criteria combine an amplitude or area drop with a limit on duration. For MMN, definite block is a fall in negative-peak area of at least 50% with no more than a 30% increase in duration. The 2021 EAN/PNS CIDP guideline uses a fall in amplitude of at least 30%, and calls a duration increase over 30% abnormal temporal dispersion.

Check the current guideline before using these numbers clinically. Cut-offs differ for the tibial nerve and for long segments.

How does temperature affect the study?

Cooling slows conduction by about 1.5–2 m/s per °C, and makes potentials larger and longer. Warm the hand to at least 32 °C, or the foot to 30 °C, before calling a nerve slow. You can change the temperature in chapter 3.

Why stimulate proximally at all?

Acquired demyelination often affects proximal segments: elbow to axilla, the plexus, and the roots (tested with F-waves). A study that stops at the elbow can miss it. The costs are more dispersion, a deeper nerve that is harder to stimulate, and less accurate distance measurements.

Axon loss or demyelination?

Most nerve diseases either destroy axons or damage myelin. Nerve conduction studies are mainly used to tell these apart and to find where the problem is.

Axon loss

. The amplitude falls at every site. Latency and velocity stay about the same, because the fastest axons are still there. The dot plot shows each axon's velocity in the forearm, and lost axons turn grey.

How slow can axon loss make a nerve?

Now . Velocity drops, because the measurement comes from the fastest axons that are left. But the slowest normal motor axons still conduct at about 35 m/s. So axon loss alone cannot lower the velocity much below 70–80% of the lower limit of normal. The response disappears first.

This is why demyelination criteria use cut-offs such as "velocity below 70% of the lower limit". Slowing beyond that means the remaining axons are conducting slowly.

Uniform or patchy demyelination

In , an inherited demyelinating neuropathy, all fibres are slowed by about the same amount. The velocity is very slow, but the CMAP keeps its shape, with no block and little dispersion.

In , an acquired demyelinating neuropathy, the damage is patchy. Different fibres are affected at different places, so proximal responses become dispersed and some fibres are blocked.

Uniform slowing suggests an inherited cause. Patchy slowing with dispersion or block suggests an acquired, usually immune, cause.

Focal lesions

An slows conduction only across the compressed segment. For the median nerve this is the carpal tunnel, which lies between the wrist stimulation site and the thumb. The distal latency is prolonged and the forearm velocity is normal. The sensory study also crosses the carpal tunnel and is often abnormal first.

For residents

What are the demyelination criteria?

The 2021 EAN/PNS CIDP guideline counts findings in at least two nerves. They include:

Distal motor latency at least 50% above the upper limit (not counting the median nerve at the wrist, because of carpal tunnel). Velocity at least 30% below the lower limit. F-wave latency at least 20% above the upper limit (50% if the distal CMAP is below 80% of the lower limit). Conduction block of at least 30%. Temporal dispersion over 30%.

The looser limits when amplitude is low follow the same logic as the 70–80% floor above: severe axon loss slows a nerve on its own.

Summarised from Van den Bergh et al., J Peripher Nerv Syst 2021. Check the source before clinical use.

Why does a normal SNAP in a numb limb matter?

Sensory cell bodies are in the dorsal root ganglion, outside the spinal canal. A lesion proximal to the ganglion (root, spinal cord) leaves the peripheral sensory axon connected to its cell body, so the SNAP stays normal even when the patient cannot feel the area. Motor neuron disease also spares sensory axons.

So: low CMAPs with normal SNAPs suggest a root, anterior horn cell or junction problem. Lost SNAPs place the lesion at or beyond the ganglion: plexus, nerve, or sensory neuronopathy.

Can an acute axonal injury look like block?

Yes. After an axon is cut, the part beyond the injury keeps conducting until Wallerian degeneration reaches it. Motor responses fade over about 3–9 days, and sensory responses a little later. In the first days, stimulating below the injury gives a normal response and stimulating above it gives none, which looks like conduction block. Repeat the study after about 10 days before calling it demyelinating.

What is sural sparing?

In early AIDP (the most common form of Guillain–Barré syndrome), the median and ulnar SNAPs are often abnormal while the sural SNAP is normal. This is the reverse of a length-dependent neuropathy, and a useful early clue. This simulator does not model sural sparing; it applies demyelination evenly along the nerve.

The safety factor at the junction

Each nerve impulse releases acetylcholine, which produces an endplate potential in the muscle fibre. Normally the endplate potential is 3–4 times larger than needed to make the fibre fire. This extra margin is called the safety factor.

The dot plot shows every endplate. Its height is the endplate potential divided by the threshold. Dots above the line fire. Dots below the line fail, and that motor unit drops out of the CMAP.

Repetitive stimulation at 3 Hz

. Each stimulus releases a fraction of the vesicles that are ready. At 3 Hz they cannot be replaced fast enough, so release falls over the first 4–5 stimuli, then partly recovers as vesicles move in from the reserve pool. In a normal junction, all endplates stay above the line, so the CMAP does not change.

Myasthenia gravis

In MG, antibodies reduce the number of acetylcholine receptors, so each release gives a smaller endplate potential. . Many endplates are now just above the line. As release falls, some drop below it and the CMAP gets smaller. The decrement is largest at the 4th or 5th response. More than 10% is abnormal.

For residents

Why 3 Hz and not faster?

Two effects compete in the nerve terminal. Leftover calcium increases release, but it clears within about 100–200 ms. Vesicle depletion lasts longer. At 2–3 Hz the calcium has cleared before the next stimulus, so you see the depletion on its own.

At 20–50 Hz the calcium effect dominates, which is why high rates are used to look for an increment. High rates are painful, though, and 10 seconds of maximal voluntary contraction does the same job.

What increment counts for LEMS?

An increment of 100% or more after brief exercise is the classic finding. A cut-off of 60% has been proposed, with high specificity and better sensitivity. Small increments (under 40%) can occur in MG and in healthy people, partly because units fire more in step (pseudofacilitation).

Oh et al., Muscle Nerve 2005.

How do you avoid missing a decrement?

Warm the limb, because cold improves transmission and can hide a decrement (the basis of the ice-pack test). Hold pyridostigmine for about 12 hours if it is safe to do so. Immobilise the limb, because movement artifact can look like a decrement. Test a weak muscle, and a proximal or facial muscle if distal studies are normal.

Single-fibre EMG is the most sensitive test. It measures the variation (jitter) in how long each endplate takes to reach threshold.

Needle EMG at rest

With the needle in a relaxed muscle, you mainly listen. Normal muscle is silent once the needle stops moving. Any ongoing activity comes from one of three sources: a single muscle fibre, a whole motor unit, or a group of muscle fibres firing together.

. You will hear the same signal that is drawn on the screen. Headphones help.

Three questions to ask

How big and how long is it? Small, brief spikes (tens of µV, a few ms) come from single muscle fibres. Larger, longer potentials (hundreds of µV to a few mV, about 10 ms) come from whole motor units.

Which way does it start? Fibrillations and positive sharp waves start with a downward (positive) deflection. Endplate spikes start upward (negative).

What is the rhythm? The firing-rate plot shows each discharge by how soon it followed the previous one from the same source. Regular sources make flat lines, irregular sources scatter, and myotonic discharges rise and then fall.

For residents

When do fibrillations appear after an injury?

Fibrillations need the muscle fibre to lose its nerve supply, which takes time. They usually appear 1–2 weeks after injury in the paraspinal muscles, and 3–4 weeks in distal limb muscles. Before then, the needle study can be normal despite a severe lesion. Fibrillations fade as fibres are reinnervated, or as they waste away months later.

How are fibrillations graded?

A common scale: 1+ persistent single runs in at least two areas; 2+ moderate numbers in three or more areas; 3+ many in all areas; 4+ filling the baseline. The grade roughly reflects how much of the muscle is denervated, not how long ago the injury happened.

When do fasciculations matter?

Fasciculations on their own are common and usually benign, especially in the calves. In motor neuron disease they occur together with active denervation (fibrillations and positive sharp waves) and chronic reinnervation (large, long motor units that fire fast because there are too few of them), in several body regions. The Awaji criteria count fasciculations as evidence of denervation when chronic neurogenic changes are present in the same muscle.
